## Factor f3 (positive): interpretation

### Preferred label  
**Lived injustice, trauma and collective repair**

This pole encodes discourse in which personal or communal suffering is narrated, connected to wider forms of inequality or institutional harm, and oriented toward healing, solidarity, accountability or reform.

### Evidence from the loadings

The 199 positive loadings form several mutually reinforcing lexical clusters:

- **Equality and social inclusion:** *equality, equity, inequity, equitable, equal, fairness, inclusion, access, discrimination, prejudice, ethnicity, sexuality, indigenous, African-American, disabled*.
- **Marginalized and vulnerable groups:** *immigration, immigrant, refugee, homeless, homelessness, gay, Syrian, Palestinians, veteran*.
- **Violence, victimization and institutional control:** *violence, violent, victim, perpetrator, assault, sexual, domestic, harassment, enforcement, armed, bail, convict, unsafe, safety*.
- **Emotional injury and recovery:** *grief, shame, trauma, suffering, harm, darkness, helpless, distress, isolation, silence, heal, resilience, empathy, loving*.
- **Collective and moral response:** *solidarity, shared, accountable, prayer, openness, initiative, leadership*.
- **Contemporary political and public discourse:** *systemic, policy, workplace, workforce, Republicans, Trump, Biden, Ukraine, disinformation, Twitter, tweet*.

The pole therefore goes beyond a general vocabulary of suffering. Harm is repeatedly framed through social categories, institutions and unequal access to safety or resources. At the same time, *heal, resilience, solidarity, empathy, shared,* and *accountable* give the pole a reparative orientation.

### Evidence from the high-scoring examples

The 2016 and 2019 examples most clearly instantiate the full loading pattern.

- The Stockton mayoral talk combines personal bereavement with structural analysis. Loading words such as *violence, victim, perpetrator, trauma, unemployment, homelessness, prejudice, ethnicity, unsafe,* and *equal* connect the murder of the speaker’s cousin to poverty, racial inequality and public policy. The proposed response is collective responsibility and institutional restructuring.
- The policing-equity talk uses *enforcement, equity, homelessness, harm, violence, safety,* and *neighborhood* to redefine racism as measurable institutional behaviour. It moves from racial grief to data-driven accountability and reform.
- The “living while Black” talk similarly uses *systemic, enforcement, armed, violence, equity,* and *equal* to expose how ordinary activities become racialized and potentially dangerous. Its solution is inclusive action and systemic change.
- The sexual-violence talks foreground *shame, confession, trauma, victim, perpetrator, silence, assault,* and *solidarity*. They are strongly testimonial, but personal disclosure is used to challenge victim-blaming, create survivor communities, encourage accountability and seek healing.
- The sex-work talk combines *equality, immigration, homeless, domestic, enforcement, violence, harm,* and *safety*. It translates lived vulnerability into a rights-based argument for decriminalization and labour protection.

The 2001 examples reveal a broader humanistic component. The poetry reading contains *grief, shame, bitter, darkness, street, sidewalk, equality,* and *heal*, presenting urban poverty, family pain, aging and social abandonment through reflective literary testimony. The mental-illness account narrates depression, treatment, confession and recovery. These texts show that the pole is not restricted to explicit political advocacy: it also includes intimate accounts of vulnerability, moral recognition and restoration.

Some isolated loading-word matches in the lower-scoring examples are semantically incidental. For example, *gay* means “cheerful” in the coaching talk, while *equal* appears in a discussion of differing abilities. Such cases should not outweigh the much more coherent convergence of the full loading list and the strongly scoring 2016–2019 texts.

## Year pattern and diachronic tendency

Two years stand out:

- **2001: mean 5.75**, but this is based on only four talks and is heavily influenced by the poetry text scoring 17. It represents an early concentration of confessional, humanistic discourse rather than secure evidence of a corpus-wide period effect.
- **2019: mean 4.31, N = 104**, the highest substantial-sample mean. The three 2019 examples strongly foreground racial justice, structural violence, policing, poverty and institutional reform, making 2019 the clearest driver of the pole.

A broader rise begins in the mid-2010s:

- 2014: 3.06  
- 2015: 3.15  
- 2016: 3.83  
- 2017: 3.75  
- 2018: 3.51  
- 2019: 4.31  
- 2020: 3.72  

By comparison, most years from 2002 to 2013 average roughly 1–3. The pattern suggests increasing prominence of discourse about structural inequality, identity-based harm, trauma and social accountability from approximately 2014 onward, peaking in 2019. Scores remain relatively elevated after 2020—generally around 3.0–3.5—although the 2019 peak is not sustained. Very early years have sample sizes too small for reliable diachronic interpretation.

## Other plausible labels

1. **Equity-oriented testimony and structural reform**  
   Emphasizes the movement from lived experience to analysis of *systemic* discrimination, policing, poverty and policy change.

2. **Social harm, accountability and healing**  
   Captures the strong co-occurrence of *violence, victim, perpetrator, trauma, shame,* and *suffering* with *heal, solidarity, empathy,* and *accountable*.

3. **Voices of vulnerability, justice and solidarity**  
   Highlights the survivor, minority and community perspectives found across the examples and the loadings for marginalized identities and collective support.

4. **Humanistic social-justice discourse**  
   Accommodates both the explicit rights-and-reform talks of 2016–2019 and the earlier literary or autobiographical treatments of grief, illness, marginality and recovery.

Of these, **Lived injustice, trauma and collective repair** is the most inclusive label: it gives appropriate weight both to the pole’s large social-justice and institutional vocabulary and to its confessional, emotional and healing-oriented examples.
